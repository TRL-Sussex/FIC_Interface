Safety Zones & Safety System

Two-tier safety: soft GUI zones that bound the robot, and hard config limits that fault it.

Overview

FIC Control Studio drives force-controlled robots, so its safety nets are first-class. The safety system operates in two tiers:

TierSourceBehaviourUse for
1 — Soft boundary GUI Safety Zones page
fic_studio_robots.yaml		 Virtual wall (restoring force via JT) at the boundary; robot stays mobile inside the zone. GUI logs a warning. User-configurable workspace restrictions, experiment fencing
2 — Hard stop robots_config.yaml
(joint_limits_deg, joint_torque_limits)		 Exceeding a config limit latches a hard fault: output drops to gravity-compensation only (hold position), control commands are refused until the robot is re-loaded. Physical actuator limits that must never be exceeded

Both tiers are verified in simulation. They are independent and always-on — the soft zones do not need to be enabled for the hard stops to work, and vice versa. The E-STOP is a third, operator-triggered layer on top of both.

Enforcement pipeline

All enforcement runs inside the controller node — the same code exists in fic_ws/src/fic_control/src/fic_control.cpp (simulation) and fic_ws/src/fic_control/src/fic_control.cpp (hardware), so behaviour is identical on both targets. Targets are sanitised at the moment they arrive (subscriber callbacks), and torques are guarded again in the 1 kHz control loop before anything is published:

Tier 1 — Soft safety zones (GUI)

The Safety page

Open Safety from the sidebar. It shows the safety zones, the workspace box, the joint limits and the velocity limits, each with a live readout of where the robot is against it. An edit is a draft until you press Review changes, read the before/after list and press Apply to the controllers; a change that makes a limit wider is marked. Applied limits are sent to every robot’s controller at once and, since v5.11.0, saved with the robot selected in the toolbar (safety_zones in fic_studio_robots.yaml, as Robot Config → Safety Zones saves them), so they are still there after a restart.

The 3D view and its toolbar

Zones in 3D draws the robot live with every zone. The toolbar on the view holds the tools:

Whole-arm safety zones

Up to 16 zones of four shapes: a box (turned about the vertical), a sphere, an upright cylinder, and an outline drawn on the floor raised between two heights. Unlike the workspace box, which bounds the tool point, a zone is checked along the whole arm: every link from the chosen joint (the second by default: the first link turns about its own axis and cannot be moved out of a zone) to the tool, each as a capsule of the link radius (50 mm by default) and the tool as a ball (30 mm), sampled every 4 cm. Set them under The arm checked. With several keep-in zones the arm must stay inside one of them; it must stay out of every keep-out zone.

The controller enforces them in every control mode (fic_library/include/fic/safety_zones.h, the same code in simulation and on hardware):

  • Virtual wall. A link past a boundary is pushed back by 2000 N/m on its deepest point, through that point’s Jacobian, as the workspace box’s wall. The arm comes to rest a few millimetres in where its own impedance pulls it towards a target inside the zone.
  • Joint targets. A joint target that would put the arm further into a zone than it is now is refused (the last target is held). A target beyond a zone is accepted when the arm can stand there; the wall then stops the arm at the zone on the way, as an obstacle would.
  • Tool targets. In the Cartesian modes the tool target is moved the least way that keeps it out of the keep-out zones and inside a keep-in zone; where no such place is found nearby, the last one that was is held.
  • Reporting. The controller publishes the arm’s worst clearance at 20 Hz; the Safety page shows it (“The arm is 34 mm from Bench”), and the Studio warns and stops on it as for the workspace box.

Warnings

When a robot touches a limit (comes within 5 mm of a safety zone or of a workspace face) or goes past it, a warning drops down from the top of the window and goes after six seconds; it stays in Notices until Clear all. Each robot and limit warns once as it touches and once more if it then goes past, and again only after it has been clear of it by 2 cm. An E-STOP, and its reset, are put in Notices too.

Stop on violation

The controller holds the robot at the limits by itself (below). Stop on violation, on by default under Monitoring, is a backstop for when something pushes the arm through anyway: when a robot is more than 30 mm past a workspace limit or a safety zone (anywhere along the arm) or 2° past a joint limit, the Studio gives that robot the graceful stop — E-STOP’s first step, the “Stop Controller” ramp-down and a hold at its current joint pose, without stopping any process. Closer than that the arm is being held at the limit (the virtual wall gives 60 N at 30 mm), so an arm resting against a limit is not stopped. Each robot is stopped once per episode: it can be stopped again only after it has come back within 15 mm (1°), so you can restart the controller and move it back in. The check runs whether or not the warn-and-log monitor is on.

Cartesian virtual wall (joint modes)

In Cartesian modes (5–8), Cartesian targets are directly clamped to the zone boundaries. In joint modes (1–4) there is no Cartesian target to clamp, so the controller uses a different mechanism:

  • A virtual wall activates when the end-effector position crosses an enabled Cartesian zone boundary. It applies a restoring force in Cartesian space (F = k × penetration, k = 2000 N/m) and projects it to joint torques via JT.
  • The wall force is zero inside the zone — no effect on normal operation. At the boundary, the robot feels a stiff spring pushing it back. Typical penetration is <5 mm for a 10 N push.
  • This replaces the previous behaviour where crossing a Cartesian zone in joint mode triggered a hard fault (broadcast stop), which was unnecessarily disruptive.

Enforcement summary (Tier 1)

  • Cartesian modes — the controller clamps every commanded EE target to the enabled zone boundaries before acting on it.
  • Joint modes — the Cartesian virtual wall adds a restoring force at the boundary. Additionally, each candidate joint target is FK-checked in the subscriber callback; if the EE would be outside the zone, the joint target is rejected (the last in-zone target is held).
  • Joint limits — joint targets are clamped to the enabled per-joint min/max angles.
  • Velocity limits — when enabled, a per-joint damping torque is added to the actuator command (see below).
  • Runtime monitor — a 10 Hz check in the GUI logs a warning when the robot is past a zone boundary, and gives it the graceful stop when it is clearly past (see Stop on violation above).

Velocity limits

Velocity limits live under Robot Config → Safety Zones and are broadcast on robot{N}_safety/velocity_limits. When enabled and max_joint_vel > 0, the controller subtracts a velocity-damping torque from each joint’s final actuator command:

tau_out = tau - (tau_limit / max_joint_vel) * joint_velocity

where tau_limit is that joint’s torque limit (fallback 150 Nm). The faster a joint moves, the more its command is opposed — a soft brake rather than a hard cut. max_joint_vel is in rad/s. The Cartesian value max_cart_vel (m/s) is received and logged by the controller but is not currently enforced.

Since v5.11.0 the same value also caps the joint planners (modes 2–4 and the H-PMP joint layer): a planned joint move never asks for more than max_joint_vel, so the damper above is not left fighting the planner (a Kinova joint in mode 2 used to plan up to 1.5708 × 1.7453 = 2.74 rad/s against a 1.0 rad/s limit). Off when the limits are disabled.

A robot's velocity block may also set sim_max_joint_vel, used instead of max_joint_vel for a robot driven in simulation (the Studio sends it to robots on the Simulation target, and the simulation node reads it at start-up); hardware always uses max_joint_vel. The Kinova Gen3 sets 3 rad/s, a third of its hardware damper gain, so large moves converge in simulation.

The GUI publishes the same limits to every active robot — a master station with several robots applies one workspace box to all of them. Until the GUI publishes, the controller boots with a default enabled box of X, Y ∈ [−1, 1] m, Z ∈ [0, 1.5] m.

Verifying limits reached the controller

The controller logs every update it accepts, e.g. Updated Cartesian safety limits: X[-0.50, 0.50] ... and Updated joint safety limits for 7 joints (enabled: true) — watch for these in the Logs. You can also inspect the wire directly: ros2 topic echo /robot1_safety/cartesian_limits (see Topics).

Tier 2 — Hard stops (robot config)

The robots_config.yaml file defines each robot’s physical actuator limits. These are not user-adjustable workspace restrictions — they are the absolute mechanical bounds of the hardware. Exceeding them triggers an immediate fault. The file the C++ nodes read lives at fic_library/config/robots_config.yaml; it is generated from fic_studio_robots.yaml by the DEPLOY TO CONTROLLER button on the Robot Config page, and the controller loads it when a robot is selected — so Tier 2 edits require a deploy plus a robot re-selection or controller restart to take effect.

Joint position limits

Defined by joint_limits_deg in robots_config.yaml:

joint_limits_deg:
  lower: [-166, -101, -166, -190, -166,  -20, -130]
  upper: [ 166,  101,  166,   -6,  166,  280,  130]

Every control loop iteration, the current joint positions (from the encoder / simulation) are checked against these limits. If any joint exceeds its config limit, the controller latches a hard fault:

  • Output torques are immediately overridden with gravity-compensation only (the robot holds its current position).
  • The fault is latched — control commands are refused until the robot configuration is re-loaded (selecting a new robot or re-starting the node clears it).
  • An RCLCPP_ERROR log identifies the offending joint, current angle, and the violated limit (HARD FAULT: Joint N at X° exceeds config limit [lo, hi]°).

These limits are distinct from the URDF/Pinocchio limits used by the FIC planner for target clamping. The planner limits prevent the controller from commanding past the bounds; the config limits catch the case where the robot actually reaches a forbidden angle (due to inertia, external forces, or a controller bug).

Per-joint torque limits

Defined by joint_torque_limits in robots_config.yaml:

joint_torque_limits:
  max: [30, 30, 30, 30, 9, 9, 9]   # Nm per joint

The output torque guard clamps each joint’s commanded torque to its own physical limit before the torque reaches the robot driver or simulator. This replaces the previous single scalar clamp (150 Nm for all joints).

Example: on a Franka FR3, joints 1–4 are clamped to ±30 Nm and joints 5–7 to ±9 Nm, matching the actuator ratings. If a robot does not define joint_torque_limits, the fallback scalar (150 Nm) is used. These same values are the hard ceiling for the autotuner’s force_max search.

E-STOP

Triggered by the Space key, the toolbar ⬛ E-STOP button, or the menu. It now stops gracefully: it fans out to every extension, then commands the controller’s mode-0 torque ramp-down to every robot and waits a brief ramp window (250 ms by default) before killing processes — so the robot decays to a safe hold instead of freezing its last torque on an abrupt kill. Each robot is then locked at its last-known joint pose and all processes are torn down. Full trigger list, teardown order, and recovery steps are on the E-STOP page.

Runtime guards

  • Per-joint torque guard — commanded torques are forced finite (NaN/Inf → 0) and clamped per-joint to the actuator rating from robots_config.yaml (falling back to 150 Nm when no per-joint limit is defined). See Tier 2 above.
  • Bounded force reflection — bilateral teleoperation haptic feedback is gain-scaled, NaN-guarded, and saturated (±30 N / ±5 N·m) so a spiking force/torque reading cannot be amplified unbounded onto the operator. See Teleoperation.

Hardware verification

Some nets can only be proven on real hardware — the controller-death watchdog, true torque saturation at the driver, and the Kinova graceful stop. These are validated separately via a written hardware test protocol (docs/safety/hardware-verification-protocol.md): a pre-flight checklist, net-by-net expected-vs-observed procedures, and a sign-off table. Everything described above is verified in simulation.

Hardware safety

Software safety limits are a last line of defence. When working with real hardware, always: start with low stiffness, keep E-STOP accessible, have a physical emergency stop within reach, and test trajectories in simulation first.
