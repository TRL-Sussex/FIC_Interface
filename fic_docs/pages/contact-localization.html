Contact Localization

Work out which link of the arm a person just pushed, using nothing but the joint torque sensors, and light that link up on a MuJoCo twin mirroring the real robot. No skin, no camera, no force/torque sensor — only proprioception.

Status: in development

The estimator and its tests are being written now. Nothing on this page has been run against a real Kinova Gen3 yet. Every number below is either measured from the shipped URDF and MJCF models, or read directly out of the source. Hardware figures will replace the model figures once the arm has been touched in anger. Read this page as a design record, not as a validated result.

The idea

A contact on link k can only load the joints proximal to it. The contact Jacobian has zero columns for every joint further down the chain, so the external torque is structurally zero on joints k+1 … 7. That makes the contacted link the most distal joint carrying significant torque — a support-pattern readout, not an optimisation.

This triangular structure is not an approximation. It was verified on the shipped Kinova model at a bent pose:

LinkJoints with non-zero Jacobian columns
Shoulder_Link1
HalfArm1_Link1, 2
HalfArm2_Link1, 2, 3
ForeArm_Link1, 2, 3, 4
SphericalWrist1_Link1 … 5
SphericalWrist2_Link1 … 6
Bracelet_Link1 … 7

Why pushes only

Restricting to pushes buys a real constraint. A push is unilateral — the force must point into the surface, so F·n ≤ 0 for the outward normal n. That inequality rejects candidate contacts which fit the torque numbers but would require pulling on the arm, and it turns the per-hypothesis fit into a constrained least squares with a closed form. No QP solver is needed.

v2: a force and a point, not just a link

v1 (above) only ever names which link was pushed. v2 goes further: for a k-joint contact it solves tau_ext = J_p(q)TF for the contact force F at a candidate surface point p, subject to the same push constraint, and reports the best-fitting point/force pair as an estimate of where and which way the push is coming from — not just which link lit up.

solve_push_force: the per-point fit

For one candidate point, hypotheses.solve_push_force(jac_point, tau_ext, normal) solves min ||tau_ext − JpTF|| subject to F·n ≤ 0. With a single inequality the optimum is either the unconstrained least-squares solution — kept as-is when it already satisfies the constraint — or lies exactly on the boundary F·n = 0. On that boundary, writing F = B y for an orthonormal basis B of the tangent plane reduces the constrained problem back to an ordinary unconstrained least squares in y. Both branches are closed-form; no iterative solver runs per candidate.

fit_contact: sweeping every link's surface

hypotheses.fit_contact(tau_ext, link_jacobians, candidates, f_max=200.0) runs solve_push_force at every decimated candidate point returned by geometry.load_candidates, across every link, and keeps the single lowest-residual hypothesis whose fitted force magnitude is under f_max newtons. It returns None outright when tau_ext itself is indistinguishable from zero — a quiet residual must never be fitted to a phantom contact. Each candidate's own body-origin Jacobian is shifted to that point first (Jp = Jlin − skew(r)·Jang, from vp = vo + ω×r).

The point is an estimate on a line, not a unique location — see Limits, item 5

With n torque sensors, a contact's location is only determined up to the force's line of action: every point along that line produces an identical tau_ext. fit_contact only ever searches the decimated candidate points it was given, so its answer is the best-fitting candidate on (or near) that line — not proof that the contact happened at that exact millimetre. Read the marker the twin draws (below) the same way: a plausible point on the line of action, not a certainty. See Limits — item 5, below — for the full statement and why no better estimator removes this.

Wiring it in: the cheap gate first

fit_contact sweeps every link × roughly 200 candidates each by default; doing that on every quiet 100 Hz tick would waste the budget for nothing. ContactNode therefore always runs the cheap v1 estimate (support_link, plus the admittance cross-check in modes 3/4) first, and only calls fit_contact once that has already reported a contact (link > 0). A new estimator parameter selects the behaviour: 'v2' (the default) runs this gated fit and populates ContactEstimate.force, .point and .residual_norm; 'v1' keeps the pre-existing link-only behaviour and leaves those three fields at their empty default. When the fit succeeds, fit_contact's own link can override the cheap gate's link number — that is the whole point of the more expensive search: a push-constrained sweep over real geometry can name a link the coarse support-pattern heuristic alone got wrong.

Frame: force and point are both converted into the robot BASE frame

fit_contact's force comes out already expressed in the same LOCAL_WORLD_ALIGNED axes ArmModel.link_jacobians uses; since this arm has a fixed base, those axes already are the base frame, so the force needs no further rotation. Its point, however, is in the contacted link's own BODY frame (as stored by geometry.load_candidates) and is carried explicitly into the base frame by ContactNode._fit_v2, using that link's current pose from the new ArmModel.link_poses(q) method: point_base = R·point_body + position. A frame mistake here is silent — nothing raises, the marker just appears in the wrong place — which is exactly why the conversion is explicit and documented rather than left implicit.

Sweeping every candidate is also not free at geometry.load_candidates's own default of 200 points per link (about 1,400 candidates total on the Kinova): measured on this development machine, fit_contact took roughly 32 ms per call at that density — over three times the 10 ms/tick budget at 100 Hz, and fit_contact runs on every tick a contact is reported, i.e. for as long as a human keeps pushing, not just once. ContactNode therefore declares its own n_per_link parameter, defaulted to 20 (measured at roughly 3 ms/call, leaving headroom for the rest of the tick), and logs the reduction from load_candidates's 200-point default at startup — a silent cap would read as full 200-point coverage when it is not.

Temporal hysteresis: the LinkDebouncer

A hand resting on a link persists for hundreds of samples at 100 Hz; threshold noise does not. hypotheses.LinkDebouncer(enter_frames=5, exit_frames=15) requires enter_frames consecutive ticks agreeing on a new link before it is reported, and — once reported — exit_frames consecutive ticks reading “no contact” before that report is cleared. Entry is deliberately faster than exit: getting the highlight on quickly matters more than getting it off quickly, and a brief dropout mid-push (one noisy tick reading below threshold) must not make the highlight flicker off and back on. ContactNode holds one instance (self._debounce) and feeds it the tick's final link estimate (v1's, or v2's when the estimator parameter refines it) right before that value is published as ContactEstimate.link_index — so fit_contact itself still runs on the raw, undebounced estimate every tick; only what gets published and highlighted waits on hysteresis.

The pre-debounce value is never discarded: it is recorded as link_est_raw alongside the debounced link_est (see data.csv columns, below), because the whole point of measuring a false-positive rate is to see what the estimator actually produced before any filter smoothed it over. A debouncer that only ever gets asked about its own filtered output cannot tell you how often it is masking a wrong instantaneous answer.

The ContactEstimate message

The contact estimator publishes {rid}_contact_estimate (100 Hz) with type communication/msg/ContactEstimate. The message carries the link location, confidence, and detailed physics of each estimate:

The link_index regimes

link_index has three distinct meanings: 1..n is the 1-based link number (proximal to distal); 0 means a real measurement of no contact (calibrated, mode 1, threshold not met); −1 means the estimator is making no claim (calibration stale, mode ≠ 1, or a residual contained NaN/inf). This distinction is critical: corrupt or unavailable data must never read as "nothing is touching the arm". Unpopulated messages default to −1, so they correctly claim no statement rather than a spurious measurement.

Why a new topic was needed

Before this work, the Studio could not see the arm's measured joint torque at all. fic_control read it at 1 kHz and threw it away. It reached ROS only as block 7 of {rid}_trajectory_data, and only in joint-admittance modes 3 and 4 — identically zero in every other mode. Worse, host.joint_effort_for() is documented as “measured joint efforts” but actually returns block 4, the FIC controller's commanded control effort. Use joint_torque_for() for the real measurement.

Calibration

The model torque is gravity only, g(q), when the arm is at rest (modes 1, 5) — valid because a push against a stationary arm is quasi-static, so there is no Coriolis term. While the arm moves under a planner (modes 2, 6) the model term switches instead to the velocity-aware C(q,dq)·dq + g(q) (Pinocchio's nonLinearEffects, one call folding the Coriolis/centrifugal term together with gravity), so that motion is not mistaken for contact. Neither form ever includes the inertial term M(q)·ddq: no measured joint acceleration exists anywhere in this project to feed it. What is left over, in either case, is absorbed by a per-pose bias: hold the pose, average tau_meas minus the model term for about a second with nobody touching the arm, and store the mean plus the per-joint standard deviation. Detection thresholds are then 6σ, floored at 1 mN·m so a noiseless calibration cannot produce a zero threshold.

That per-pose bias is the whole trick. It cancels model error and stiction at that configuration together, which is what lets the estimator work below the controller's own hand-tuned 2.0 N·m admittance_deadbands.joint_tau. The calibration goes stale when the arm leaves the pose, and a stale calibration is reported rather than silently used — see “Control mode”, below, for how modes 2 and 6 relax that check, and what it costs.

Calibration is triggered by calling the {rid}/contact/calibrate service (std_srvs/Trigger) while the arm is held still and nobody is touching it. The node accumulates joint torque for calibration_seconds (default 1 s, i.e. 100 ticks at 100 Hz) before deriving the bias, noise and per-joint thresholds; no contact estimate is published while a capture is in progress. The call fails immediately (success: false) if no joint feedback has arrived yet.

Recording a run

Calling the {rid}/contact/record service (std_srvs/Trigger) toggles recording: the first call opens a run folder and starts buffering one row per tick; the second call flushes it to disk and clears the buffer. Nothing is written to disk until the second call, so a run of any practical length costs memory, not open-file overhead. Recording has no calibration prerequisite of its own — it buffers whatever the node is doing, blocked ticks included — but a run with no calibration will simply show every tick blocked with calibration stale/not calibrated and every per-estimate column empty, which is itself a useful (if boring) recording.

Each run is its own folder:

Absence is not zero

A channel with no sample on a given tick is written as an empty CSV cell and named in meta.json's missing_channels list if it never produced a single sample across the whole run — never as a fabricated 0. A zero is a real measurement in this project (zero torque, zero admittance offset, a genuine “no contact” verdict), so writing one where nothing was actually measured would be indistinguishable from that real reading. This governs every optional column above: tau_ext/link_est/confidence/link_est_raw on a blocked tick, adm_offset outside joint admittance, and link_truth until the operator declares one. fic_contact/recorder.py's Recorder enforces this mechanically — a key simply absent from the row dict it is given stays absent all the way to disk — and its own test suite (test/test_recorder.py) pins the behaviour, so it cannot silently regress.

Limits — read this before demonstrating it

These are properties of the sensor set, not defects, and no better estimator can remove them.

1. Two links are structurally invisible

  • Base_Link is bolted to the world, so a push on it loads no joint at all. Seven detectable classes, not eight.
  • Bracelet_Link and the gripper are one class — anything distal to joint 7 excites all seven joints identically.

2. The proximal links need implausible force

A push is sensed only through its moment arm about the joints proximal to it, and the proximal links sit close to their own joint axes. Measured over 150 random poses × real MJCF surface points × random push directions, against a 1.2 N·m detection floor:

LinkForce detected 50% of the time…90% of the timePractical verdict
Shoulder_Link69 N426 Nnot by hand
HalfArm1_Link24 N66 Nmarginal
HalfArm2_Link9.6 N30 Nfirm push
ForeArm_Link4.3 N9.7 Ncomfortable
SphericalWrist1_Link3.6 N7.2 Ncomfortable
SphericalWrist2_Link3.2 N5.6 Ncomfortable
Bracelet_Link3.0 N5.5 Ncomfortable

So the honest claim is five links comfortably, HalfArm1 marginally, and Shoulder not at all. For a demonstration, push the forearm or the wrist. Pushing the shoulder will look broken when it is in fact behaving correctly.

3. Whole geometric families of pushes are invisible, at any force

The torque a contact exerts about joint i is axis₀ · (r × F), where r is the lever arm from the joint axis out to the contact point. That vanishes in three separate ways, and all three happen on this arm:

  • r = 0 — the contact lies on the joint axis. Nothing is sensed, in any direction.
  • F parallel to the axis — the push runs along the axis.
  • r parallel to the axis — the lever arm runs along the axis instead of radially out from it.

Shoulder_Link at q = [0, 0.5, 0, 1.5, 0, 1.0, 0] shows all three. Its body origin sits exactly on joint 1's axis, so its entire linear Jacobian is zero and a 20 N push there gives tau = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0] — exactly zero on all seven joints, for any force direction. Moving out to the real surface only partly helps: joint 1's axis is Z, so a 6 cm offset along X yields just 1.2 N·m from a 20 N push, while the same offset along Y or Z yields exactly zero.

Over random poses, real surface points and random directions at 20 N against a 1.2 N·m floor, the invisible fraction is 100% on Shoulder_Link, 54% on HalfArm1_Link, 16% on HalfArm2_Link, and under 3% from ForeArm_Link outward.

This is the joint-torque-only singularity the literature warns about — distinct contacts producing identical sensor readings. See References.

4. Single contact is assumed

Two hands on the arm at once will produce a plausible-looking single answer that is wrong. Multi-contact separation is what a base force/torque sensor buys you, and this arm does not have one.

5. v2's point is one estimate on a line, not a unique location

Read this before trusting the marker

With n torque sensors, a contact force is only observable up to its moment about each joint — and every point along the force's line of action produces the exact same moment, hence the exact same tau_ext. No amount of additional fitting recovers information the sensors structurally never captured: v2's fit_contact can only ever pick the best-fitting point among the decimated candidates it was given, which is a plausible point on that line — not proof the contact happened there rather than 2 cm further along the same line, on a neighbouring link's surface, or anywhere else the line passes through solid geometry. Treat the twin's marker sphere the way you would treat confidence on a v1 link estimate: informative, not certain. This is not a bug to be fixed by a better solver — it is the same joint-torque-only observability limit as items 2 and 3 above, applied to position instead of to link identity.

Simulated accuracy vs. noise

The tables above are noise-free: real surface points and directions, but a clean tau_ext. fic_contact/sim_validation.py adds the missing dimension — injected Gaussian torque noise — so a single measured hardware noise floor sigma turns into a per-link accuracy prediction instead of a number with no context.

cd fic_ws/src/fic_contact
python3 -m fic_contact.sim_validation --trials 200 --out /tmp/accuracy_vs_noise.csv

Method, per noise level × per link × --trials repeats: a random pose, a random point drawn from that link's full mesh vertex set — every stored vertex, not load_candidates' decimated search set — and a random push direction with F·n < 0. This matters more than it looks: pushing exactly at a candidate fit_contact already searches is a best case that never happens on a real hand, and scoring only that case would inflate v2's reported accuracy. The same (pose, point, direction) scenarios are reused across every noise level for a given link, so only the injected noise changes the outcome from one row to the next.

Output is accuracy_vs_noise.csv (sigma_nm, estimator, link, trials, correct) plus a small summary figure saved next to it. To read the CSV against a measured hardware noise floor: find the row whose sigma_nm is closest to your measured sigma, and correct / trials for each link at that row is the expected accuracy at that noise level — not a pretty curve, a lookup.

Read those two cells as "v2 fits whatever it is given," not as evidence the proximal links are usable.

v1's low sigma=0 numbers on links 3–7 are a real finding, not noise-free perfection failing to reproduce. support_link reports the most distal joint whose own torque clears threshold. A point sampled from the full mesh near a link's own proximal attachment has a small lever arm about that link's own driving joint while still producing a large, above-threshold torque on the joints further up the chain (a longer accumulated offset) — so v1 under-reports distality on exactly the points a real hand is just as likely to land on as any candidate. fit_contact's full seven-dimensional least-squares fit is not fooled by this, because it matches the whole torque pattern rather than checking one threshold crossing at a time; verified independently against a finite-difference Jacobian. This is the same "v1 fails near a joint axis" limitation already pinned by test_v1_fails_when_the_force_line_passes_near_a_joint_axis, showing up far more often under unbiased random sampling than in that test's hand-chosen vector.

As sigma rises, both estimators degrade on links 3–7, and v2 stays ahead of v1 at every level there — by 5 to 22 percentage points at sigma = 1.2 N·m, this plan's own working detection-threshold figure. On Shoulder_Link/HalfArm1_Link both sit within noise of 0% throughout the sweep; where v1 shows a few points more than v2 there, it is v1's laxer single-joint criterion occasionally firing by chance on a signal too weak for v2's stricter geometric fit to accept — not a genuine edge, and consistent with item 2's force-needed tables. Full numbers: accuracy_vs_noise.csv and the figure beside it.

Admittance: which joint is yielding

Admittance control is exactly the case “which link got pushed” needs to answer for a human leaning on the arm — the arm is designed to give way, and the question is where. Modes 3 and 4 (joint admittance) publish a second, independent observable on {rid}_admittance_offset, so the node now computes two per-joint signals and cross-checks them:

  • Torque residual (the same tau_ext = tau_meas - g(q) - b the impedance modes use, via ResidualEstimator.residual().)
  • Admittance offset — {rid}_admittance_offset, N float64s in radians, published only while an admittance mode (3 or 4) is active. This is fic::ficControl::humanOffset(i) read straight off the controller: the same offest accumulator handleJointAdmitanceControl (fic_control.h) integrates the human's push into every control tick, exposed verbatim rather than reconstructed. Fed through hypotheses.support_link exactly like the residual is, via fic_contact.admittance.admitting_link (see fic_contact/admittance.py), because the same triangular argument applies: a contact on link k can only push joints 1…k away from their target, so the most distal joint displaced names the link just as well as the most distal joint loaded does.

When the two agree on a link, that cross-check is reported as high confidence; when they disagree, confidence is derated rather than silently picking a side — see fic_contact.admittance.combine_link_estimates.

Why the offset can see a push the residual missed — and why not by magic

The controller's own admittance integrator is gated by a hard 2.0 N·m admittance_deadbands.joint_tau deadband (on the Kinova): a push that never clears 2.0 N·m on any control tick produces exactly zero offset, full stop — the offset is not immune to that floor, it sits behind the same one. What it adds over one fresh residual sample is memory: admittance mode 1 (Push-and-Stay) accumulates the offset as a running integral, so once a push clears the deadband on even a single tick, the resulting nudge persists, where an instantaneous residual reading forgets it the very next tick. “Integrates over time” means retains a push it once saw, not detects pushes below its own floor.

Why {rid}_admittance_offset replaced the {rid}_trajectory_data blocks

Contact localization originally derived this observable as q_target_adm - q_target from blocks 5 and 3 of {rid}_trajectory_data. That turned out to be wrong: in modes 3/4, handleJointAdmitanceControl never writes the pre-push target to block 3 at all — block 3 there is the trajectory planner's own copy of the already admittance-adjusted target (block 5), smoothed or clamped. So the block-5-minus-block-3 difference was the planner's own tracking lag, not the human's push — large while a joint's target is actively moving, decaying toward zero the moment the push holds steady, even though the human is still leaning on the arm. {rid}_admittance_offset is the fix: it is the controller's own integrated human offset (fic::ficControl::humanOffset(), the offest accumulator itself), published directly instead of reconstructed from two downstream copies of the target. fic_contact/admittance.py's parse_trajectory_blocks and admitting_joints still exist — and are still correct — for anyone parsing a raw trajectory_data recording offline, and its module docstring keeps the full trace, but contact_node.py's live decision path no longer calls them.

Cartesian admittance (7, 8) is weaker, on purpose

Cartesian admittance never routes through handleJointAdmitanceControl, so fic::ficControl::humanOffset() is never populated in modes 7/8 and neither fic_control publishes {rid}_admittance_offset in them (see each publisher's own mode gate). There is no per-joint offset to read, so modes 7 and 8 fall back to the torque residual alone: a real, working, single-observable estimate, just without the cross-check that makes modes 3/4 stronger.

Blocks 6/7 are not used for the torque observable

fic_control.cpp calls the admittance controller with the measured torque negated (current_effort_ = -joint_torques_[i]), so block 6 minus block 7 works out to g(q) + tau_meas — a sum, not a zero-mean residual. Reusing it would risk biasing or inverting the link decision, so the node computes the torque observable the same way in every mode: independently, via ResidualEstimator.residual() against {rid}_joint_torques/{rid}_q_current.

Accuracy is best at rest — modes 1 and 5, where the model term is gravity only, g(q), exactly as it was calibrated. Every other mode moves the arm — under a planner (2, 6) or under admittance (3, 4, 7, 8) — so the model term switches to the velocity-aware C(q,dq)·dq + g(q) instead, computed from {rid}_joint_velocities. Neither term ever includes the inertial term M(q)·ddq: no measured joint acceleration exists anywhere in this project to feed it, so a moving-mode estimate is always missing a term a stationary one is not. That gap, together with the wider calibration pose tolerance a moving mode needs (below), is made visible rather than hidden: confidence — for both observables, before they are combined — is derated for joint speed and for calibration pose drift, so a moving-mode estimate is never reported at the same confidence a fresh, stationary one would earn.

The mode check is one of four gates the node runs every tick, in order, before it will publish anything but link_index = -1:

GateRefuses when…
joint feedbackno {rid}_joint_torques or {rid}_q_current sample has arrived yet.
control modethe last {rid}_control_mode value is not one of 1–8.
calibrationno bias has been captured via {rid}/contact/calibrate.
pose driftthe arm has moved more than pose_tolerance from the calibration pose — the bias is stale. In every moving mode (2, 6, 3, 4, 7, 8) this tolerance is deliberately relaxed (ContactNode.MOVING_POSE_TOLERANCE), since the arm leaves the calibration pose almost immediately once it starts moving; the resulting staleness is carried instead in a derated confidence, not hidden.

This logic lives in ContactGate, a small ROS-free class in fic_contact/gate.py so it can be unit-tested without a graph; contact_node.py only wires its inputs and reads blocked_reason() once per tick. A bare ContactGate() still refuses all four admittance modes by default — contact_node.py explicitly passes the wider ContactNode.ALLOWED_MODES (all eight) to opt in.

The twin viewer

fic_contact/twin.py opens a MuJoCo window that mirrors the real arm's measured pose and lights up whichever link {rid}_contact_estimate currently names. It subscribes to {rid}_q_current and {rid}_contact_estimate; it publishes nothing.

A display, not a simulation

Every tick the twin writes the measured joint angles straight into d.qpos and calls mujoco.mj_forward — it never calls mj_step. Stepping physics would fight the mirror: the simulated arm would fall under gravity while the twin is trying to hold it at the real arm's pose. mj_forward only recomputes kinematics and derived quantities for the pose just written in; it runs no integrator.

Launch it with the installed twin entry point:

# macOS -- mujoco.viewer.launch_passive requires mjpython on the main thread
mjpython $(ros2 pkg prefix fic_contact)/lib/fic_contact/twin --xml <scene.xml> --robot-id robot1

# Linux -- plain python works
python3 $(ros2 pkg prefix fic_contact)/lib/fic_contact/twin --xml <scene.xml> --robot-id robot1

--joints and --links default to the Kinova Gen3 7-DOF set (Actuator1…7 and the seven link names in the table above) but can be overridden for a different serial arm, in keeping with the rest of this package being robot-agnostic. Both are always resolved by name (mj_name2id + jnt_qposadr/body_geomadr), never by position — the Kinova scene has nq = 13 (seven arm joints plus six Robotiq gripper joints), so indexing qpos positionally would silently read the wrong slots.

Highlighting follows the link_index regimes

The twin highlights a link only when link_index ≥ 1. 0 (a real measurement of no contact) and -1 (no statement possible) both leave every link its normal colour — the geom-id lookup used to choose which link to paint simply has no entry for either value, so no special case is needed to keep them unhighlighted.

The v2 contact marker: a point and a push direction

Lighting up a whole link answers which link was pushed; it says nothing about where on that link, or which way the push is coming from. When the estimator parameter is 'v2' and ContactEstimate.point is non-empty, the twin draws two extra geoms into MuJoCo's viewer.user_scn — a scratch scene the passive viewer merges into the render on sync(), kept entirely separate from the model's own geoms so drawing into it can never disturb the arm itself:

  • A small yellow sphere (mjGEOM_SPHERE, 1.5 cm radius) at ContactEstimate.point, the fitted contact location in the base frame.
  • A cyan arrow (mjGEOM_ARROW, via mjv_connector) running from a point offset behind the contact — along the reverse of the push — up to the marker itself, so the arrowhead lands exactly on the contact point. Its length is a fixed 8 cm on screen, not a Newton-to-metre scale: a scale picked for a light touch would send a hard push's arrow off the arm, and one picked for a hard push would make a light touch invisible. The arrow shows only the push direction; never read its length as the push's strength.

Both geoms are drawn by twin._draw_contact_marker(scn, link, point, force), which resets scn.ngeom = 0 every frame before drawing (so a marker from an earlier tick with a fit never lingers on a tick with none) and draws nothing at all unless link ≥ 1 and point is present — exactly the same v1/v2 and no-contact/no-statement guards the link highlighting already follows.

The marker is an estimate on a line, not a certainty

See Limits, item 5, above, before reading anything into the marker's exact position: with n torque sensors a contact's location is only determined up to the force's line of action, so the sphere sits on a plausible point on that line — picked because it also lies on the searched surface geometry — not on a uniquely determined contact point.

Launching the viewer from the Studio

The top toolbar's ▶ VIEW button, next to ▶ PLT, starts and stops fic_contact/twin.py as a managed subprocess — no terminal, no manually copying the robot's mujoco_xml_path. It used to be a ◊ Contact Twin action inside the control bar; it moved to the toolbar and was renamed Viewer so the label reads as what it launches, and is now visible only while the Studio's control target is Hardware — mirroring the real arm only makes sense while driving the real arm. main_window_pro._refresh_toolbar_launch re-applies that visibility on every target change, the same hook that already keeps the Simulation/Hardware/VR launch cluster in sync. Clicking it while stopped launches the viewer for the active robot (mjpython on macOS, plain python3 on Linux, per-run log under /tmp/fic_contact_twin_<timestamp>.log), and the button itself flips to ■ VIEW while running, matching every other process toggle in that bar; clicking it again terminates the process, falling back to pkill -9 -f fic_contact.twin if it does not exit within 3 seconds. A robot whose config has no mujoco_xml_path logs a warning instead of launching — there is nothing to render. Closing the Studio (or a crash) reclaims the viewer the same way it reclaims the simulator and controller processes.

The ◊ Contact panel — still in the control bar, unmoved — holds a Calibrate button, which calls the active robot's {rid}/contact/calibrate service (hold the arm still and clear of contact first), and a Ground truth combo listing none plus the seven link names. Its live readout beside the mode/rate indicator (below) follows the same hardware-only visibility rule as the Viewer button.

The ground-truth combo: how it reaches the node

Selecting an entry in the Ground truth combo sets the active robot's contact node's link_truth ROS parameter, via that node's own standard set_parameters service — never by the Studio reaching into a ContactNode Python object directly, which was never actually possible once the GUI and the node are separate processes. ContactNode declares link_truth (default 'none') and registers an add_on_set_parameters_callback that resolves a link name to the corresponding ArmModel.link_names index and calls set_link_truth(); 'none' clears the declared truth back to no ground truth declared, kept distinct from the operator declaring no contact (link 0, which the combo never offers) — the same distinction set_link_truth's own docstring insists on. Once a link truth is declared this way, a recorded run's data.csv finally gets a populated link_truth column instead of an empty one — the entire point of recording it in the first place.

The control bar's live link readout

A readout beside the mode/rate indicator shows the active robot's {rid}_contact_estimate at 5 Hz, visible only while the control target is Hardware — the same gate the toolbar's Viewer button follows, applied in ControlBarMixin._cb_apply_availability rather than _refresh_toolbar_launch since the readout itself never left the control bar. It renders four visually distinct states — the same link_index regimes as above, plus the case the geom highlighting does not have to distinguish: no message at all.

ReadoutMeaning
--No {rid}_contact_estimate message has ever arrived — the contact node is not running, or has not published yet. Not the same as a link reading; there is simply no data.
UNCALlink_index == -1: gated off (uncalibrated, wrong control mode, stale calibration, or no joint feedback) — no statement about contact is possible.
no contactlink_index == 0: a genuine measurement — the node is calibrated, gated on, and nothing is touching the arm.
a link name and confidence, e.g. ForeArm 87%link_index in 1..n: the estimated contacted link (_Link suffix dropped for width) and its confidence, rounded to the nearest percent.

These four states must never collapse into one another — absence, an uncalibrated estimator, and a genuine "nothing is touching the arm" reading are three different facts, and looking exactly alike would make the difference invisible to whoever is watching the bar.

  • fic_ws/src/fic_control/src/fic_control.cpp — publishes {rid}_admittance_offset for the simulated arm too

residual, hypotheses, geometry, recorder, gate and admittance import no rclpy, no PyQt5 and no fic_studio, so the whole estimator is testable with neither a robot nor a GUI. That purity is enforced by an AST test, not by good intentions.

geometry.load_candidates() samples each link's candidate points by a uniform stride over that link mesh's native vertex order — not random sampling, so a run is reproducible — keeping at most n_per_link of them (the function's own default is 200), each with its outward normal carried along and transformed into the same body frame the Jacobians use. The Kinova link meshes run from 4,593 vertices (ForeArm_Link) to 11,637 (Bracelet_Link) at roughly 5–6 mm mean vertex spacing, so even a few hundred candidates per link sample far more finely than the estimator can resolve (see Limits, above). ContactNode itself overrides that default down to 20 via its own n_per_link parameter — see “Wiring it in: the cheap gate first”, above, for why 200 was too slow for the 100 Hz tick budget.

Running the tests

ROS 2's launch_testing pytest plugins are incompatible with the fic_ros environment's pytest 9.1.1, so a bare pytest crashes before collecting anything. Use:

PYTEST_DISABLE_PLUGIN_AUTOLOAD=1 python3 -m pytest test/ -q
